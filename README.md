# Peilung

Peilung is a field navigation kit for Apple Watch and iPhone, for finding your way on foot when hiking, geocaching or orienteering. It answers the questions you ask on a trail: which way to go, how far you have come, where you are, and where you have been. A compass that can take and hold a bearing, navigation to a point, a distance odometer, your coordinates and saved marks all live in one app, on the wrist or in the hand.

The watch app and the iPhone app do the same things and each works on its own: the watch app needs no iPhone nearby, and neither needs an account or a network. Each keeps its own marks and odometer; the goal being navigated and the settings are shared, so a goal set or stopped, or a setting changed, on either device shows on the other. There are no ads and no analytics, and Peilung does not start a workout, so it never interrupts one you are recording in another app.

## Using it

The app's pages scroll vertically with the Digital Crown, and it reopens on the page you last used.

**Navigate** is a compass until you give it somewhere to go: the ring turns so its N points north, and the amber mark at the top is the direction the watch points. Once it has a goal, an amber arrow inside the ring points the way. It takes you to a point or along a bearing. For a point, the arrow aims at it and turns green when the watch points within a few degrees of it, and the page gives the distance and bearing, and how far to turn when it lies well off to one side. The arrow follows the watch's compass where it has one; without one, the ring is drawn north-up with the bearing marked. Within about ten meters the page shows a green flag and "Near target" instead of an arrow, since GPS noise outweighs direction at that range, and the watch taps once to say you have arrived; it taps again only after you have moved well away and come back. The scope button at the top right enters coordinates by hand: the arrows pick a field, the Digital Crown changes it, and every digit wraps on its own, so changing the last few digits of your current position never disturbs the rest. A line under the coordinates shows how far and which way the entered point is from you as it changes, and Go saves the point to Marks before navigating to it, so typed coordinates are never lost.

The button at the top left takes a bearing. Point 12 o'clock at a landmark and press Lock, or turn the Digital Crown to dial one read off a map, then Follow it. The arrow shows the bearing relative to where the watch points; it turns green and the watch taps once when you are within a few degrees, and when you drift off it taps a rising pattern to turn right or a falling one to turn left. On bearing taps at once; drifting off has to hold for a second with the screen up before it taps, so lowering the wrist after checking the bearing does not set off a turn cue. When a GPS fix was available as you took the bearing, the page also shows how far you are to the left or right of the line and how far along it, which catches the sideways drift a compass alone cannot see after you step around an obstacle. A watch compass is far rougher than a baseplate compass, so the page warns when the watch reports a poor heading and asks for the figure-8 calibration motion. Tapping the goal's name at the top of the page turns it into a Stop button for a few seconds; tapping that stops navigating, for a point or a bearing, and returns to the compass. A goal set or stopped on the paired iPhone or watch reaches the other the next time Peilung runs there, opening it on Navigate, and the later change wins.

Bearings are shown against magnetic north by default, because orienteering maps are drawn to it, so a bearing measured off one can be dialed in as is. The watch's compass reads magnetic north with or without GPS, so taking and following a bearing needs no fix. The North setting switches to true north, which needs the local declination: the watch learns it from its first GPS fix and remembers it, and until then readings stay magnetic and are labelled `M`. GPS bearings to a point are true, and show as `T` while the declination is unknown.

GPS-based navigation is not allowed during most orienteering competitions; check an event's rules before using Peilung in one.

**Odometer** is a stopwatch for distance. Press Start where you are, walk or run, and come back to it whenever you like to see how far you have gone and for how long. The distance comes from the watch's own step count, which it records whether or not Peilung is open, so the odometer costs no battery between glances and survives the app being closed. Arm movement while sitting still counts as a few steps, so a distance under about 20 steps shows as a dash. watchOS turns steps into distance with a stride length it learns from outdoor walks and runs recorded with GPS, as long as Motion Calibration & Distance is on in the iPhone's Location Services, System Services; an outdoor walk in the Workout app calibrates a new watch. When a GPS fix was available at Start, the page also shows the straight-line distance back to where you started, and tapping that line navigates there.

**Here** shows your position, with its accuracy, and a Mark button that saves it. A position the watch remembered from earlier is shown dimmed with its age until a fresh fix arrives, and only a fresh one can be marked.

**Marks** lists saved points with their age and distance from here. Tap one to navigate to it, swipe to rename or delete it. The settings live at the bottom of this list: units, the north bearings are shown against, and the coordinate format, which Here, Marks and coordinate entry all follow. A setting changed on the paired iPhone or watch reaches the other the next time Peilung runs there. Coordinates default to the degrees and decimal minutes geocaching uses (`N 29° 45.123`), and can instead be degrees, minutes and seconds (`N 29° 45′ 07.4″`) or decimal degrees (`N 29.75205°`).

A complication opens Peilung from the watch face, in the circular, corner, rectangular and inline slots. While navigating, each shows where the last fix stood against the goal, taken as the screen dimmed or the app closed: on a bearing, how far left or right of the line, and on the way to a point, the distance. The complication cannot read the compass or the GPS itself, so the reading does not move until the app is opened again, and the rectangular and inline slots say how old it is. The rectangular one, which is also the Smart Stack widget, adds the goal and, on a bearing, the distance along the line; without a reading it shows the distance tracked since Start, which the watch refreshes about every quarter hour and whenever the app changes it.

**On iPhone** the pages are tabs instead of vertical pages, and the rest follows from holding a phone instead of wearing a watch. The top edge of the iPhone stands in for 12 o'clock, so the compass reads the way the top of the phone points; hold it flat, screen up, in portrait. Where the watch takes the Digital Crown, the iPhone takes a drag up or down on the number or the coordinates, or the buttons below them, and the turn cues play as growing taps for right and fading ones for left. The odometer counts the iPhone's own steps, so it tracks distance only while the phone is carried. The complication is the watch's alone.

GPS runs only while Peilung is on screen and the display is not dimmed, and on the iPhone it stops when the phone locks. Raising the wrist within the watch's Return to Clock time brings it back where you left it, and a fresh fix follows within seconds. That time is two minutes unless changed; setting it to an hour for Peilung alone, under Settings, General, Return to Clock on the watch, keeps the app on the wrist through a hike or a run.

## Building

Open `Peilung.xcodeproj` in Xcode. The `Peilung` scheme builds the iPhone app with the watch app embedded, and archives the two for the App Store; the `Peilung Watch App` scheme builds the watch app alone for a watch or the simulator and runs the tests.

Both apps compile `Shared`, which holds the models and the four pages. `Peilung` and `Peilung Watch App` hold what differs by device: the coordinate and bearing entry sheets, the haptics, and the wording and button style in `Support/Platform.swift`.

The identity a fork changes, meaning the team id, the bundle ids and the display name, lives in `Config/Peilung.xcconfig`, and the version in `Config/Version.xcconfig`. The project has no dependencies beyond Apple's frameworks.

The complication is its own extension target, `PeilungWidgets`, embedded in the watch app. The two share an App Group, set in `Config/Peilung.xcconfig` and named again in `Shared/Support/Store.swift`, where the app leaves the state the widget shows.

A simulator has no pedometer, so a simulator build walks at a steady pace instead. A Debug build launched with `--demo` and a scene name replaces Core Location with a fixed position and compass and fills in a running odometer, marks and a goal, for screenshots; adding `--entry coordinates` or `--entry bearing` opens that entry sheet. The scenes are listed in `Support/Demo.swift`. A Debug build launched with `--haptics` plays each navigation cue in turn with its name on screen, which is the way to feel them on a device.

## Privacy

Peilung makes no network connection. It reads your location while it is on screen and not dimmed, and your step distance while the odometer runs. On each device it keeps the odometer reading, your marks, the current target, its settings, the page last shown and the last magnetic declination the compass reported. What leaves the device is the current goal and the settings, sent to Peilung on the paired iPhone or watch through Watch Connectivity. The privacy policy is at https://excelano.com/legal/#peilung.
